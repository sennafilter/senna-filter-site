Most bad AI answers come from a thin prompt. "Write me a bio" leaves Claude guessing about who you are, who's reading and how long it should be. The fix isn't a magic phrase. It's giving the same few pieces of information every time, so Claude doesn't have to guess.

## New to the words?

- **Prompt.** What you type to Claude. It can be one line or several paragraphs.
- **Context.** The background Claude can't know unless you say it: who you are, what you're working on, who it's for.
- **Output format.** The shape of the answer you want, like a table, three bullets or an email.

# 1. Say who you are and what the job is

~ One sentence of context changes the whole answer.

**What it is.** Start with your role and the task. This tells Claude the level to pitch the answer at.

**Example.** Instead of "explain this contract", try "I run a two-person design studio and I'm not a lawyer. Explain this contract in plain English."

> **Start here.** Begin every prompt with "I'm a [role] working on [task]."

# 2. Give it the real material

~ Paste it in. Don't describe it.

**What it is.** The actual text, numbers or notes you're working from. Claude does better with your real draft than with your summary of it.

**Why it matters.** When you describe a document, you leave out the details that matter. When you paste it, Claude sees them.

# 3. Say who it's for and how it should sound

~ The audience decides the tone.

**What it is.** Name the reader and the feel. "For a skeptical manager who has five minutes" gets a different answer than "for a friend".

**Make it better.** Paste a sample of your own writing and say "match this tone."

# 4. Ask for a specific shape

~ Length, format and what to leave out.

**What it is.** Tell Claude how the answer should look so you don't have to reformat it.

- "Three bullets, each under 15 words."
- "A table with columns for option, cost and risk."
- "An email under 120 words."
- "No jargon and no intro paragraph."

# 5. Invite questions

~ The cheapest way to avoid a wrong answer.

**What it is.** Let Claude ask before it starts. This is especially useful for anything complicated.

```
Before you answer, ask me up to three questions if anything is unclear.
```

> **Pro tip.** Answer the questions in one message, then say "go". You get a tailored answer in two steps instead of three rounds of fixing.

## The template

Copy this and fill in the brackets. You can delete lines that don't apply.

```
I'm a [role] working on [task].

Here's the material:
[paste it]

This is for [audience], and it should sound [tone].

Give me [format and length]. Leave out [what you don't want].

Before you answer, ask me up to three questions if anything is unclear.
```

## Before and after

**Before:**

```
Write a LinkedIn post about my new job.
```

**After:**

```
I'm a marketing coordinator who just started at a healthcare software company. Write a LinkedIn post announcing it. Audience: former coworkers and recruiters. Tone: warm and specific, not corporate. Under 100 words, no hashtags, and no phrases like "thrilled to announce". Ask me two questions first if you need anything.
```

The second one takes thirty seconds longer to write and saves ten minutes of editing.

## When the answer is still off

- **Too long?** "Cut this in half. Keep the numbers."
- **Too stiff?** "Rewrite this like I'm explaining it to a friend."
- **Wrong direction?** "That's not what I meant. Here's what I'm after: [one sentence]."
- **Made something up?** "Which parts of that are you not sure about? Mark them."

## Check your prompt

[ ] I said who I am and what I'm doing.
[ ] I pasted the real material.
[ ] I named the audience and the tone.
[ ] I asked for a specific format and length.
[ ] I let Claude ask me questions first.
